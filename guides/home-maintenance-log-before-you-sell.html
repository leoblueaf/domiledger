Home maintenance log: what to record before you sell

Buyers can't see years of care. They see the house on one afternoon and read an inspection report. A clear maintenance history helps fill in the rest.

A good log won't replace an inspection, and it won't make an old roof new. What it does is answer the questions every buyer, agent and inspector asks: how old is it, when was it last serviced, and who did the work?

What to record

1. Major systems and their ages

Roof, furnace or heat pump, air conditioner, water heater, electrical panel, and plumbing updates. Record the install year, brand, model and who installed it. "The water heater is from 2019" is far more reassuring than "I think it's newer."

2. Routine maintenance

HVAC service visits, gutter cleaning, dryer vent cleaning, water heater flushes, chimney inspections, septic pumping, pest treatments. The date and a one-line note are enough. Regular entries show a pattern of care.

3. Repairs and replacements

What broke, what was done, who did it, what it cost, and any warranty on the work. Keep the invoice. If a problem was fixed properly, a record of the fix is much better than leaving a buyer to wonder.

4. Improvements and permits

Renovations, additions, new windows, electrical or plumbing work. Note whether a permit was pulled and closed. Buyers and lenders sometimes ask.

5. Appliance details and transferable warranties

Model and serial numbers, purchase dates, and warranties that transfer to a new owner. Some roofing, window and HVAC warranties can transfer, sometimes with a fee or a deadline, so check the terms.

6. The small stuff buyers love

  • Paint colors and finishes by room
  • Filter sizes for the HVAC and refrigerator
  • Where the water, gas and electrical shutoffs are
  • Trusted contractors and service companies

How to organize it

  • Chronological by system is the easiest for a buyer to read: roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, appliances, exterior.
  • Keep receipts with the entries, not in a separate shoebox.
  • Start now, even if you're not selling. A log started years ahead is complete; one assembled the week before listing is a scramble with gaps.
Disclosure rules vary by state. A maintenance log is a helpful supplement, not a substitute for required seller disclosures. Ask your agent or attorney what you're required to disclose.
